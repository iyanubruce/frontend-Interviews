# Common Mistakes to Avoid for Front End System Design Interviews

> Source: [common-mistakes](https://www.greatfrontend.com/front-end-system-design-playbook/common-mistakes)

Unlike coding interviews which are much more common and you can somewhat practice/simulate interview conditions, it is much harder to practice for system design interviews.

Here's a list of common mistakes made by candidates when they are answering system design interview questions and many of them are not specific to front end system design interviews. Hopefully you will remember these points and avoid them for your own interviews.

## Jumping into answering the question immediately

Don't jump into answering the question immediately! Take the time to gather requirements and clarify assumptions by asking questions.

> **Clarify the product requirements before choosing an architecture**
>
> System design prompts are deliberately under-specified. Diving in without scoping the problem often leads to a polished answer for a product the interviewer never had in mind, which is one of the fastest ways to fail this round.

## Approaching the question in an unstructured manner

Because system design interviews are very open-ended with no explicit completion milestone (unlike coding interviews where you have to produce working code), some candidates might just talk about whatever that comes to their mind and the answer might end up appearing messy to the interviewer. Use the RADIO framework to help you.

Write down each step of the RADIO framework on the whiteboard at the start of the interview and ensure that by the end of the interview you have covered each section sufficiently. Note that you don't necessarily have to complete each section in order and you can always revisit previous sections if you've missed out on certain things.

## Insisting on only one solution or the best solution

Don't insist there's only one solution, especially if the interviewer prompts you for alternative approaches. More often than not, there are multiple ways to solve a problem, each with its own tradeoffs.

The interviewer wants to see you identify a solution with the right tradeoffs for the issue at hand, not you saying that there's only one right or best solution. The other solutions might be clearly bad and are very obvious to you, but the interviewer wants to hear you explain why they are bad.

## Remaining silent the entire time

On the opposite of the spectrum of answering too quickly, there are people who remain too silent. Don't remain silent the entire time and thinking only in your head – think out loud!

System design interviews are meant to be collaborative exercises between you and the interviewer. Treat your interviewer as a coworker, bring up issues you've identified, bounce ideas, and discuss possible solutions with them.

## Going down a rabbit hole

Don't go down a rabbit hole of diving too deep into a particular component. Define an initial architecture/high-level design first, then proceed to elaborate on the various parts of the system. Focus on the parts which are most important to the problem.

If you are unsure, ask the interviewer if you should dive deeper into a specific component. It'd be bad to spend too much time discussing an unimportant component, wasting precious time without providing useful signals to the interviewer.

## Using buzzwords without being able to explain them

Don't use buzzwords or terms you can't explain well. It might be tempting to throw out some cool terms like "Virtual DOM", "DOM Reconciliation", "Partial Hydration", "Streaming Server-side Rendering" and you can if they're relevant to the current topic!

However, if you do, make sure you are able to explain the term you just used as your interviewer is likely going to probe for more information to test your knowledge.

> **Use terms you can explain in a follow-up question**
>
> Interviewers routinely probe buzzwords to test depth. Being unable to explain "Virtual DOM" or "Partial Hydration" after using it is a clear signal of surface-level knowledge and is harder to recover from than simply not mentioning the term.

## Exercises

### Check your understanding 1

You notice that you began discussing implementation details before establishing scope or structure. Which actions help recover? Select all that apply.

- [x] Pause, acknowledge the missing scope, and ask focused questions about requirements and priorities.
  > This corrects the root problem because a design cannot be judged until both parties agree on what is being built.
- [x] Write down the RADIO phases, summarize the agreed requirements, and establish a high-level architecture before choosing a deep dive.
  > This restores structure because the checklist exposes missing areas and the architecture provides context for later detail.
- [ ] Continue the current low-level topic so changing direction does not look indecisive.
  > This compounds the mistake because time is still being spent on a detail whose importance has not been established.
- [ ] Privately assume the product requirements and avoid involving the interviewer.
  > This risks answering the wrong question because the interview prompt is intentionally under-specified.

### Check your understanding 2

Which behaviors help a candidate explain technical decisions and discuss alternatives with the interviewer? Select all that apply.

- [x] Use a relevant technical term only when you can explain how it works and why it affects this design.
  > This demonstrates depth because the concept is connected to an architectural decision and can withstand follow-up questions.
- [x] Think aloud, compare alternatives, and ask whether the interviewer wants a deeper exploration of the important component.
  > This is collaborative because it makes reasoning visible while aligning limited time with the interviewer's priorities.
- [ ] List Virtual DOM, partial hydration, and streaming SSR without explaining or applying them.
  > This signals surface knowledge because unsupported terms invite follow-up questions the candidate cannot answer.
- [ ] Insist on a single solution and dismiss requests to discuss tradeoffs.
  > This weakens the answer because system design problems usually admit several approaches whose suitability depends on context.
